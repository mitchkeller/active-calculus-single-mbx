Example 8.2.6. The degree \(5\) Taylor polynomial of \(f(x) = \ln(1+x)\).

Find the degree \(5\) Taylor polynomial centered at \(a = 0\) for the function \(f(x) = \ln(1+x)\text{.}\)
🔗
Solution.
To find the degree \(5\) Taylor polynomial, we need to compute \(f(0)\text{,}\) \(f'(0)\text{,}\) \(f''(0)\text{,}\) \(\ldots\text{,}\) \(f^{(5)}(0)\text{,}\) so we first find the first through fifth derivatives of \(f\) in the first several rows of Table 8.2.7, and then evaluate those derivatives at \(a = 0\) in the last several rows.
🔗
When finding the coefficients of a Taylor polynomial, it is often helpful to not combine products such as \((-3)(-2)(-1)\) and \((-4)(-3)(-2)(-1)\) into a single number, in order to better observe patterns; indeed, by not combining the constants that arise in higher derivatives of \(f(x) = \ln(1+x)\text{,}\) we see patterns of alternating signs and factorials that arise. From the last six rows of Table 8.2.7 and the fact that \(c_k = \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\text{,}\) we find that
\begin{align*} c_0 \amp= f(0) = 0 \\ c_1 \amp= f'(0) = 1 \\ c_2 \amp= \frac{1}{2!}f''(0) = \frac{-1}{2!} = -\frac{1}{2} \\ c_3 \amp= \frac{1}{3!}f'''(0) = \frac{(-2)(-1)}{3!} = \frac{1}{3} \\ c_4 \amp= \frac{1}{4!}f^{(4)}(0) = \frac{(-3)(-2)(-1)}{4!} = -\frac{1}{4} \\ c_5 \amp= \frac{1}{5!}f^{(5)}(0) = \frac{(-4)(-3)(-2)(-1)}{5!} = \frac{1}{5} \end{align*}
so that the degree \(5\) Taylor approximation of \(f(x) = \ln(1+x)\) at \(a = 0\) is
\begin{equation*} T_5(x) = 1x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3 - \frac{1}{4}x^4 + \frac{1}{5}x^5\text{.} \end{equation*}
Thus, we have found the approximation
\begin{equation*} \ln(1+x) \approx 1x - \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \frac{1}{3}x^3 - \frac{1}{4}x^4 + \frac{1}{5}x^5\text{,} \end{equation*}
and by plotting \(T_5(x)\) along with \(f(x)\) and \(T_1(x)\) in Figure 8.2.8, we see how much better the degree \(5\) approximation is than the tangent line approximation.
